THE 8 STUDY METHODS
1. Active Recall
Psychological evidence Active recall is one of the most extensively researched study
methods in cognitive psychology. Roediger and Karpicke's landmark 2006 study published
in Psychological Science found that students who tested themselves on material retained
50% more one week later than students who simply reread their notes. This is called the
testing effect — the act of retrieving information from memory actually strengthens the
memory itself. The harder your brain works to pull something out, the stronger that neural
pathway becomes. Rereading by comparison creates a false sense of familiarity without
building real retrieval strength.
How it helps students study better and more efficiently Instead of spending an hour
rereading the same page, a student spends 20 minutes being tested on it and retains
significantly more. It also immediately shows them what they don't know, so they stop
wasting time on what they already have and focus on the actual gaps. For students
preparing for AP exams or SATs where recall under pressure is everything, this is the most
directly applicable method.
Who it suits based on the quiz Students who say they understand material while studying
but go blank on the test. Students who currently reread notes or make flashcards. Students
studying math, science, or history where factual retrieval is required. Students who say
they learn best by testing themselves.
Step-by-step directions for real life
1. Read through your notes once, normally.
2. Close or flip over your notes so you cannot see them.
3. On a blank piece of paper or in a blank document, write out everything you can
remember from what you just read.
4. Open your notes and compare. Circle or highlight everything you missed or got
wrong.
5. Focus your next study pass only on what you missed.
6. Close your notes again and test yourself a second time.
7. Repeat until you can retrieve everything without looking.

2. Blurting
Psychological evidence Blurting is a direct application of the generation effect, first
documented by Slamecka and Graf in 1978. Research consistently shows that information
you generate yourself is remembered significantly better than information you simply read.
When you force yourself to write out everything you know without looking, your brain
must work harder to construct the memory, and that effort is what makes it stick. It also acts
as a retrieval practice exercise, which shares the same evidence base as active recall.
How it helps students study better and more efficiently Blurting is fast. A student can
blurt everything they know about a topic in under two minutes. It immediately reveals
exactly where the gaps are without needing a teacher or a quiz. It is also a zero-setup
method since all you need is a blank page, which makes it one of the most accessible
methods for any student in any situation.
Who it suits based on the quiz Students who say they do not know what to focus on or
where to start. Students who feel overwhelmed by how much there is to study. Students
studying vocabulary-heavy subjects like languages or science. Students who say they
understand material in class but it disappears by test day. Students who prefer writing over
speaking.
Step-by-step directions for real life
1. Read through your notes or a section of your textbook.
2. Close everything completely.
3. On a blank piece of paper, write down every single thing you can remember about the
topic. Do not stop, do not look, do not edit. Just write.
4. When you run out of things to write, open your notes.

5. Underline or circle everything in your notes that you did not write down.
6. Read only the things you missed.
7. Close your notes again and blurt a second time, trying to add what you missed.
8. Repeat until your blurt matches your notes.
Step-by-step how it works in the app
1. Student pastes their notes into the app.
2. App shows the topic or subject title only, not the notes.
3. A blank text box appears with the prompt: write down everything you know about
this topic without looking.
4. Student types freely for as long as they want.
5. When they press done, the app compares their blurt to the original notes using AI.
6. App highlights the key concepts they covered in green and the ones they missed in
red.
7. Student can review what they missed and blurt again.
8. Each round their score improves showing them visible progress.
3. Practice Testing
Psychological evidence Practice testing has the strongest evidence base of any study
method. Dunlosky et al.'s 2013 landmark review in Psychological Science in the Public
Interest rated practice testing as high utility, the highest rating given, and rated rereading
as low utility, the lowest. Taking practice tests under realistic conditions, meaning timed
and without notes, is the closest simulation to what a real exam demands. Research also
shows that students who practice test consistently significantly outperform students who
study more but test themselves less, even when total study time is equal.
How it helps students study better and more efficiently Practice testing shows students
exactly what a real test will feel like, which reduces test anxiety because the actual test no
longer feels unfamiliar. It also forces retrieval under pressure, which is the exact skill being
tested. Students who only study passively are essentially training for a different game than
the one they are being graded on.
Who it suits based on the quiz Students who say they do not know if their studying is
working. Students who experience test anxiety. Students who need a score or measurable
progress to stay motivated. Students studying any subject since practice testing works
universally across all content types. Students preparing for standardized tests like the SAT
or AP exams.
Step-by-step directions for real life

1. Find or create practice questions for your topic. Use past tests, textbook review
questions, or generate your own.
2. Set a timer for a realistic amount of time, the same as the real test if possible.
3. Put your notes away completely and attempt every question without looking anything
up.
4. When the timer ends, stop even if you are not finished.
5. Grade your own work against your notes or an answer key.
6. For every question you got wrong, write out the correct answer in your own words.
7. Do another round of practice testing on only the questions you got wrong.
Step-by-step how it works in the app
1. Student pastes their notes into the app.
2. At the end of each study session, the app generates a five question mini quiz from the
material covered.
3. Quiz is timed, one to two minutes per question.
4. Student answers each question without being able to see their notes.
5. When submitted, the app grades the quiz and gives a score out of five.
6. Correct answers are shown in green. Wrong answers show the correct answer
alongside the student's answer.
7. Score is saved so the student can track improvement across sessions.
8. Any question answered wrong is flagged to come back in the next session.
4. Feynman Technique
Psychological evidence The Feynman Technique is rooted in elaborative encoding, which
is one of the most well-established principles in cognitive psychology. Research by Craik
and Lockhart in 1972 showed that information processed at a deep semantic level, meaning
you engage with meaning rather than surface features, is retained far longer than
information processed shallowly. Explaining something in your own simple words forces
deep processing. The generation effect also applies here since you are constructing the
explanation yourself. Additionally research on peer teaching consistently shows that the
act of teaching something consolidates the teacher's own understanding.
How it helps students study better and more efficiently The Feynman Technique
immediately exposes the difference between recognizing something and actually
understanding it. A student can read the definition of natural selection five times and feel
like they know it. The moment they try to explain it simply without looking, they discover

exactly where the understanding breaks down. This saves time by targeting real gaps
rather than giving students a false sense of preparation.
Who it suits based on the quiz Students who say they understand material in class but
cannot explain it in their own words. Students studying humanities, biology, economics, or
any conceptual subject. Students who say they learn best when things make sense rather
than when they are memorized. Students who struggle with essay questions or free-
response sections on tests.
Step-by-step directions for real life
1. Choose one concept from your notes, just one.
2. Put your notes away.
3. On a blank page write or say out loud: explain this concept as if you are teaching it to a
10-year-old who has never heard of it.
4. Write or speak freely until you run out of things to say.
5. Look back at your notes and find where your explanation was vague, wrong, or
incomplete.
6. Go back and study only those gaps.
7. Try the explanation again from scratch.
8. You understand the concept when you can explain it simply without any gaps or
vague language.
Step-by-step how it works in the app
1. Student pastes their notes into the app.
2. The app identifies the key concepts in the notes and picks one to start with.
3. A prompt appears: explain this concept in your own words as if you are teaching it to
someone who has never heard of it.
4. Student types their explanation freely in a text box.
5. The AI reads the explanation and compares it to the original notes.
6. App responds with specific feedback: what the student got right, what was missing,
and what was unclear.
7. Student can try again with the feedback in mind.
8. Once the explanation is solid the app moves to the next concept.
5. Concrete Examples
Psychological evidence The concreteness effect is a well-documented phenomenon in
cognitive psychology. Research by Paivio's dual coding theory, developed in the 1970s and

extensively validated since, shows that information paired with a concrete image or real-
world example is encoded in two ways simultaneously: verbally and visually. This double
encoding significantly improves recall. Abstract information with no concrete anchor is
much harder for the brain to retain because it has no hook to attach to. Research also shows
that understanding a principle through an example transfers better to new problems than
memorizing the principle alone.
How it helps students study better and more efficiently Rather than trying to memorize
an abstract definition, a student who understands a concrete example of that concept can
reconstruct the definition themselves, and also recognize the concept when it appears in a
different form on a test. This is especially powerful in math and science where formulas and
theories need to be applied, not just recalled.
Who it suits based on the quiz Students who say abstract concepts confuse them. Students
studying math, science, economics, or philosophy where abstract principles are common.
Students who say they connect to real life examples better than textbook definitions.
Students who feel frustrated because they can read something multiple times and still not
understand it.
Step-by-step directions for real life
1. After reading a concept or definition, stop and ask yourself: what is a real-world
example of this?
2. The example should be something you have actually seen, heard, or experienced, not
another textbook example.
3. Write the concept, then below it write your example.
4. Ask yourself: how does my example show the concept? Be specific.
5. If you cannot find an example, you do not understand the concept yet. Go back and
reread.
6. Test yourself: read only the example and try to state the concept from it without
looking.
Step-by-step how it works in the app
1. Student pastes their notes into the app.
2. After each concept card the app generates a real-world example or analogy that
illustrates the concept.
3. The example is shown after the student has attempted to recall the concept
themselves.
4. Student reads the example and the app asks: how does this example show the
concept?
5. Student types a brief answer.

6. App confirms whether their connection is correct or clarifies where it missed.
7. For math and science the app generates a sample problem as the concrete example
and walks through why it applies.
6. Pomodoro Timer
Psychological evidence The Pomodoro Technique is grounded in research on cognitive
fatigue and attention. Attention research by Ariga and Lleras published in 2011 in Cognition
found that brief mental breaks significantly improve sustained attention over longer
periods. The brain is not designed for sustained deep focus for hours at a time. Research on
the vigilance decrement shows that focused attention begins to degrade after 20 to 40
minutes without a break. Structured rest periods reset this and allow the next work session
to begin at full cognitive capacity rather than diminished capacity.
How it helps students study better and more efficiently A student who studies for two
hours while gradually losing focus retains far less than a student who does four focused 25-
minute sessions with breaks. The Pomodoro method also removes the psychological
resistance to starting because the endpoint is always visible. It is easier to commit to 25
minutes than to commit to studying until you are done.
Who it suits based on the quiz Students who say they get distracted and lose focus quickly.
Students who say they check their phone while studying. Students who do not have a real
study pattern. Students who feel overwhelmed by how much material there is. Students
who struggle to start studying at all.
Step-by-step directions for real life
1. Put your phone on do not disturb and place it face down or in another room.
2. Open only the tab or material you need. Nothing else.
3. Set a timer for 25 minutes.
4. Study with complete focus until the timer goes off. If a distraction pops into your head,
write it down and ignore it until the break.
5. When the timer goes off, stop immediately even if you are mid-sentence.
6. Take a real five minute break. Stand up, walk around, drink water. Do not scroll your
phone.
7. After the break, start a new 25-minute round.
8. After four rounds take a longer 20-minute break.
Step-by-step how it works in the app
1. When the student starts a study session the Pomodoro timer activates automatically
in the background.

2. A small progress bar at the top of the screen shows how much time is left in the
current session.
3. The student scrolls through their study cards normally while the timer runs.
4. At 25 minutes the app pauses automatically and shows a break screen with a fun fact
or subject-related meme.
5. A five minute countdown runs on the break screen.
6. When the break ends the app resumes the session automatically.

7. Confidence Rating
Psychological evidence Confidence rating is a metacognitive study tool. Metacognition,
which is thinking about your own thinking, is one of the most well-researched areas in
educational psychology. Flavell's foundational work in 1979 established that students who
accurately monitor their own understanding learn significantly more effectively than
students who do not. Research by Dunning and Kruger also shows that students who lack
metacognitive skills tend to dramatically overestimate how well they know material, which
leads them to stop studying too early. Confidence rating forces students to accurately
assess their own knowledge after each piece of material, which leads to better allocation of
study time.
How it helps students study better and more efficiently Confidence rating stops students
from spending equal time on things they already know and things they do not. It
immediately redirects attention to material that needs more work and gives the student
real-time data about where they are weakest. It also reduces the Dunning-Kruger effect by
making overconfidence visible in the data.
Who it suits based on the quiz Students who do not know what to focus on or feel
overwhelmed by the amount of material. Students who run out of time studying and guess
incorrectly about what to prioritize. Students who feel confident before a test and still do
poorly. Students who want to feel in control of their own study session.
Step-by-step directions for real life
1. After testing yourself on any piece of material, immediately rate your confidence on a
simple scale: easy meaning you knew it instantly, medium meaning you got it but
were not sure, or hard meaning you did not know it or got it wrong.
2. Keep a list of all your hard and medium ratings.
3. Your next study session should begin with everything rated hard, then medium.
4. Everything rated easy gets reviewed briefly at the very end.
5. Re-rate after each review and adjust accordingly.

8. Elaborative Interrogation
Psychological evidence Elaborative interrogation was studied extensively by Pressley,
Symons, McDaniel, Snyder, and Turnure in 1988, and follow-up research has consistently
validated it. The method works by activating prior knowledge and forcing the brain to
connect new information to what it already knows. Research shows that asking why-
questions during studying significantly improves retention compared to reading the same
material without questioning. The mechanism is that answering why forces the brain to
process information at a deeper semantic level and integrate it into existing knowledge
structures rather than storing it in isolation.
How it helps students study better and more efficiently Students who ask why something
is true understand the logic behind a fact, not just the fact itself. This means they can
reconstruct the answer on a test even if they cannot remember it exactly, because they
understand how it follows from something they do know. It also makes studying more
engaging because it turns passive reading into active sense-making.
Who it suits based on the quiz Students who say they want to understand why something
is true rather than just memorize it. Students studying history, biology, economics, or any
subject with cause and effect relationships. Students who say they forget things that do not
make sense to them. Students who enjoy thinking deeply about ideas. Students who are
curious and find rote memorization boring.
Step-by-step directions for real life
1. Read a fact or concept from your notes.
2. Immediately ask yourself: why is this true? Do not move on until you have an answer.
3. If you do not know why, look it up. Then ask why again.
4. Also ask: how does this connect to what I already know about this topic?

5. Write your why-answer below the fact in your notes.
6. Later when you review, try to reconstruct the fact from the why. If you remember the
reason, you can usually reconstruct the fact even without memorizing it directly.
Step-by-step how it works in the app
1. Student pastes their notes into the app.
2. After a concept or fact card, the app generates a why-question: why do you think this
is true, or how does this connect to what came before?
3. Student types their answer in a text box.
4. The AI evaluates the answer and responds with whether the reasoning is correct, what
was missing, and any additional connections the student may have missed.
5. Student can read the full explanation if they are stuck.
6. The question and the student's answer are saved so they can review their reasoning
later.
SUBJECT TO METHOD MAPPING
Math and Science Primary: Active Recall, Practice Testing, Concrete Examples Secondary:
Elaborative Interrogation, Confidence Rating Reason: These subjects require accurate
retrieval of formulas and procedures, application to new problems, and deep understanding
of why methods work.
History and Humanities Primary: Feynman Technique, Elaborative Interrogation, Blurting
Secondary: Active Recall, Confidence Rating Reason: These subjects reward understanding
connections and causes, being able to explain concepts in your own words, and recalling
large amounts of narrative information.
Language Primary: Active Recall, Blurting, Confidence Rating Secondary: Practice Testing
Reason: Language learning is heavily vocabulary-based which requires fast, accurate
retrieval of words and their meanings, with confidence rating helping students know which
words need more repetition.
English and Writing Primary: Feynman Technique, Elaborative Interrogation, Concrete
Examples Secondary: Blurting Reason: English rewards deep understanding of texts and
ideas, the ability to articulate arguments clearly, and connecting abstract themes to
concrete evidence.
Multiple Subjects Primary: Pomodoro Timer, Confidence Rating, Active Recall Secondary:
Blurting Reason: Students with multiple subjects need structure and time management
above everything else, with confidence rating helping them prioritize across subjects.

HOW THE SCORING WORKS
Each answer adds points to one or more of the 8 methods. At the end of all 7 questions the
app ranks all 8 methods by total score and recommends the top two or three. It never
recommends just one because most students benefit from a combination. The subject from
Q1 also applies a multiplier that boosts subject-appropriate methods. For example a student
studying history who cannot explain things in their own words and gets frustrated would
receive Feynman as their primary method, elaborative interrogation as their secondary, and
blurting as a quick daily habit. The app then explains why each was recommended using
specific language that reflects what the student actually answered, not generic
descriptions.
